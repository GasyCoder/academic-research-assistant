# Academic writing review criteria

Detailed criteria for step 4 of the [Academic Writing workflow](../SKILL.md#workflow). Each
dimension lists what to look for and what to avoid doing. Not every passage needs every
dimension: pick those that match the request.

Apply the [severity definitions](../SKILL.md#severity) by impact on meaning and understanding.
Missing context in an excerpt calls for a question, not an automatic critical finding.

## Clarity

Look for:

- ambiguous sentences with more than one plausible reading;
- vague wording where a precise term exists (*some aspects*, *various factors*, *things*);
- pronouns and demonstratives (*this*, *it*, *they*) whose referent is unclear;
- syntactic overload: nested clauses, long noun stacks, several ideas in one sentence;
- the main point buried at the end of a long sentence or paragraph.

Avoid: splitting every long sentence. A long sentence with a clear structure is fine.

## Precision

Look for:

- overgeneralisation (*always*, *never*, *all*, *proves*, *clearly shows*) beyond the evidence
  presented;
- causal wording (*causes*, *leads to*, *results in*, *due to*) where the text only shows an
  association or a sequence;
- key terms used before being defined, or used with shifting meanings;
- claims whose scope (population, setting, period, conditions) is not delimited;
- statements presented as established facts with no visible support (citation may be needed).

Avoid: removing hedges that express real uncertainty; adding hedges to every sentence.

## Concision

Look for:

- repetition of the same idea or word at short distance;
- pleonasms and redundant pairs (*future prospects*, *each and every*);
- inflated phrases (*in order to* → *to*, *due to the fact that* → *because*);
- metadiscourse that says nothing (*it should be noted that*);
- the same transition repeated across paragraphs.

Avoid: cutting conditions, limitations, hedges, definitions or the information a reader
needs to reproduce or evaluate the work. Repetition of a technical term may preserve its
referent: do not replace it with a less precise synonym simply to vary the vocabulary.

## Coherence

Look for:

- missing logical link between consecutive sentences or paragraphs;
- abrupt topic changes;
- apparent contradictions between statements;
- connectors that do not match the relation (*therefore* without a consequence, *however*
  without a contrast);
- terms or abbreviations used inconsistently.

## Register

Look for:

- conversational wording, contractions, rhetorical questions where unusual for the genre;
- promotional or emotional language (*remarkable*, *alarming*, *perfect*);
- unjustified intensifiers (*very*, *extremely*, *highly significant* when no test is reported);
- value judgements presented as findings.

Avoid: replacing clear words with jargon or making sentences longer to sound academic.

## Argumentation

For relations relevant to the passage, check that their connection is understandable:

- claim → justification or evidence;
- problem → evidence that it is a problem;
- result → interpretation, and interpretation → result it rests on;
- hypothesis → rationale;
- comparison → explicit basis and conclusion.

These are examples, not a required research structure: a passage need not have hypotheses,
participants, quantitative data, or separate Results and Discussion sections. When support
is not visible, ask whether it appears elsewhere before treating the omission as a defect.
Never supply the missing evidence, data or source.

## Paragraph structure

Look for a main idea, its development, support (evidence, example, citation, data), and a
link to what follows. Short transitional or one-sentence paragraphs can be appropriate;
do not force a single template on every paragraph.

## Language notes

Principles are the same in every language. LanguageTool covers grammar and spelling of the
language it is given; these notes cover conventions it does not judge.

### English

- Keep one spelling variant (US or UK) consistently; check with `en-US` or `en-GB` accordingly.
  Some British styles accept *-ize*; follow the stated house style rather than treating
  every *-ize* spelling as American or incorrect.
- Common hedges: *may*, *might*, *suggests*, *indicates*, *appears*, *likely*, *potentially*.
- *We* is common in many fields; impersonal or passive constructions in others. Keep the
  author's choice unless asked.
- Avoid contractions in formal academic prose.

### French

- Typography: non-breaking spaces before `:` `;` `?` `!` and inside « guillemets »; LanguageTool
  flags many of these, and they are minor issues.
- Common hedges: *semble*, *pourrait*, *suggère*, *tend à*, *vraisemblablement*, *environ*.
- *Nous* (often of modesty) and impersonal constructions are both used; keep the author's choice.
- Watch for anglicisms and calques when a precise French term exists, but keep established
  technical terms of the field.
- In either language, judge sentence structure by clarity, not a presumed national preference
  or a fixed target length.
